# The Industrial Landscape

**Data as of 3 September 2026.** Every figure in this chapter comes from a
company filing, an operator's own published statement, or the U.S. Geological
Survey, and each row of the table below names its source. This chapter is the
shortest in the book and by a wide margin the most perishable: several of the
plants listed are in construction or commissioning, and any reader more than a
year past that date should treat the table as a starting list of operators
rather than as current numbers.

Who actually separates rare earths, where, and at what scale. The structural
fact behind all of it is concentration. The USGS puts 2025 world mine
production at 390,000 t of rare earth oxide, of which China accounts for
270,000 t — about 69 % [@usgs2026mineral]. Separation and refining are more
concentrated still than mining ([](#supply-chain-concerns)), and it is the
separation step, not the mining step, that every project below is a response
to.

## Two Companies, One Country

The Chinese industry is not merely large; since 2024 it has been legally
consolidated into two entities. China's Ministry of Industry and Information
Technology allocates the mining and the smelting-and-separation quotas that
govern legal output, and eligibility for those quotas is now restricted to
**China Rare Earth Group** and **China Northern Rare Earth (Group) High-Tech**,
down from six companies previously. The most recent publicly reported quota
figures are 270,000 t for mining and 254,000 t for smelting and separation; the
2025 allocations were issued without public announcement, and companies were
reportedly instructed not to disclose them
([Mining Technology, 21 July 2025](https://www.mining-technology.com/news/china-rare-earth-quotas-2025/),
reporting Reuters). No nameplate separation capacity is published for either
group in any source verified for this chapter, which is itself the salient fact:
the largest separation capacity in the world is also the least documented.

China Northern Rare Earth operates on the Bayan Obo bastnäsite-monazite
resource at Baotou in Inner Mongolia and dominates the light rare earths;
China Rare Earth Group is centred on the southern ion-adsorption clay
provinces, which is where the heavy rare earths come from
([](#ion-adsorption-clays)). The distinction matters commercially, because it
is dysprosium and terbium — not neodymium — that no non-Chinese operator
produced at scale until 2025.

## How to Read That Table

**Nameplate is not output.** MP Materials states an anticipated separated-REO
capacity of about 20,000 t/y including roughly 6,075 t/y of NdPr oxide, and in
2025 produced 2,599 t of NdPr oxide — a record for the company, more than
double the prior year, and still explicitly below design capacity, with full
design rates expected in Q1 2027. Energy Fuels commissioned a circuit rated at
850-1,000 t/y of NdPr by running 500 t of monazite through it and making 38 t
of oxide. Neither is a failure; both are what ramping a separation plant looks
like. A separation train is a long countercurrent cascade
([](#solvent-extraction-fundamentals)), and a cascade does not reach steady
state quickly, does not tolerate a variable feed, and does not produce on-spec
product until every stage has filled. Read every capacity number in the table
as a design intent and every production number as a measurement.

**Most operators do not publish a nameplate at all.** Lynas, Solvay, Neo, SRC,
and both Chinese groups publish no separation capacity figure that could be
verified for this chapter. Where the column says "not published", that is a
statement about the public record, not an estimate withheld. Any table you
encounter that fills those cells with numbers is drawing them from analyst
estimates or trade press, and should say so.

**The heavy rare earths are the real bottleneck.** Nearly every operator in
the table can, or plans to, make NdPr. The list that can make separated
dysprosium and terbium is much shorter, the tonnages are two to three orders
of magnitude smaller — Lynas at 19 t of Dy and Tb in a quarter, Energy Fuels
planning 35 t/y of Dy and 12 t/y of Tb, Iluka designing for about 750 t/y of
combined Dy and Tb — and the feed for them is ion-adsorption clay or
xenotime-bearing monazite, not bastnäsite ([](#ion-adsorption-clays)). This
is why the 2025 export controls named the heavy elements
([](#supply-chain-concerns)) and why several projects in the table added heavy
circuits after 2024.

**The price signal is uneven across the series.** USGS-reported 2025 average
prices were about \$69/kg for 99 % NdPr oxide, \$27/kg for europium oxide and
\$30/kg for gadolinium oxide, against \$1.00/kg for lanthanum oxide and
\$1.71/kg for cerium oxide [@usgs2026mineral]. A plant that separates the full
slate must sell the light end at commodity prices to recover the cost of the
separation it performed on the whole of it, which is a large part of why the
economics of a new entrant are so much worse than the flowsheet suggests
([](#environment-techno-economics-and-life-cycle)).

## What Changed Recently

The years 2025 and 2026 were the first in which the non-Chinese industry did
something other than announce projects. Lynas produced separated samarium
oxide for the first time in March 2026 and lifted dysprosium and terbium
output to 19 t in the June 2026 quarter; Solvay inaugurated a magnet-grade
line at La Rochelle in April 2025; Neo opened a magnet plant in Narva with a
dysprosium and terbium oxide line in September 2025; MP Materials doubled NdPr
output and began magnet manufacture at its Independence facility in Texas,
having committed under its July 2025 agreement with the U.S. Department of War
to expand that facility to a projected 3,000 t/y of magnets and to add samarium
separation at Mountain Pass; Energy Fuels made kilogram quantities of
99.9 % dysprosium oxide; and Rare Element Resources started its Wyoming
demonstration plant in March 2026.

None of that changes the concentration figure yet. The tonnages involved are
small against 390,000 t of world mine production [@usgs2026mineral], and the
capacity that exists outside China is still weighted heavily toward NdPr from
light-rare-earth feeds. What did change is that the number of independent
non-Chinese operators able to make a separated heavy rare earth oxide went from
zero to more than one, which is the step that matters for the argument made
throughout this book: the chemistry was never the constraint
([](#research-directions-and-open-questions)).
