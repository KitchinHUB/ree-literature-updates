# Explore the Bibliography

The [Bibliography](#bibliography) lists every work this book cites, in one
alphabetical run. That is the right shape for checking a citation and the wrong
shape for every other question. This page is the other shape: pick an element,
an extractant, a technique, a feedstock, or a kind of work, and the
bibliography narrows to what matches.

It is worth knowing how the tags were made, because it tells you what the
filters can and cannot be trusted to do.

**Extractant, technique and feedstock are matched against text.** A controlled
vocabulary of about sixty terms — the same one the [Index](#index) is built
from — is matched against each work's title, keywords, abstract, and, for the
578 works whose full text was collected during fact-checking, the body of the
paper. A term has to appear in the title or keywords, or at least four times in
the body, to count. This is mechanical and therefore consistent, and it is why
these three facets are the ones to lean on.

**Element and topic were assigned by reading titles.** Matching element names
against the bibliography alone finds a specific element in only 122 of 758
works, because a paper about praseodymium is usually titled "separation of rare
earth elements." Counting mentions in the full text recovers most of the rest,
but counting cannot tell the difference between a paper *about* dysprosium and
a paper that mentions it once while listing what else is in the ore, and it
cannot tell a mechanistic study from a flowsheet design at all. So those two
facets were assigned per work, by hand, against the title and venue. Expect the
occasional wrong call.

**An empty result is often a real result.** Much of this literature treats one
element pair with one extractant, and a combination that returns nothing is
frequently a combination nobody has published on. That is not the same as
nothing existing, and it is worth checking against the chapter text before
concluding anything — but it is not always a bug in this page either.

Selecting a filter puts it in the page's URL, so a narrowed view can be
bookmarked or sent to someone.
