## G24 — the chapter's central argument was backwards

The intro claimed separation "by boiling point" with the rare earth chlorides as
the volatile species. They are the non-volatile species. Rewrote the opening so
the argument is *the impurities leave, the rare earths stay*, and added a
property table (SiCl₄, POCl₃, TiCl₄, AlCl₃, FeCl₃, ZrCl₄, ThCl₄, CaCl₂, LnCl₃)
with melting and boiling/sublimation points, sourced to the CRC Handbook
[@haynes2016crc], Seifert's critical review of LnCl₃ melting points
[@seifert2005melting] and Brunetti's LaCl₃ vapour-pressure work
[@brunetti2000vaporization].

Two honesty notes are written into the table caption: the LnCl₃ boiling figure
(≳1,550 °C) is an extrapolation of Brunetti's equation log(p/kPa) = 9.65 −
13,989 K/T far beyond its measured range (1,137–1,188 K) and is uncertain by
well over 100 K; and per-element LnCl₃ melting points are given as a range
(≈580 Tb to 930 Lu) because Seifert's per-element table is paywalled and I would
not fabricate 17 values.

Also added the point the chapter never made: nothing in a 600–900 °C chlorinator
distinguishes NdCl₃ from PrCl₃, so carbochlorination is a competitor to acid
baking, not to solvent extraction. That framing now runs through the chapter and
closes the Outlook.

## Thermodynamics fixed

The chapter asserted that direct halogenation of rare earth oxides is
thermodynamically unfavourable. It is not: La₂O₃ + 3 Cl₂ → 2 LaCl₃ + 1.5 O₂ has
ΔG° ≈ −290 kJ at 298 K and stays negative to ~1,000 K. Rewrote to say what is
actually uphill — CeO₂, TiO₂, ZrO₂, Al₂O₃ — and what carbon actually does
(removes the product-side oxygen and drives the reaction past the oxychloride
intermediate), cited to [@pomiro2021panoramic].
